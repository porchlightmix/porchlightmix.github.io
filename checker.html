What this check measures

Integrated loudness
The average loudness of the whole song in LUFS, measured the way streaming services do (ITU-R BS.1770-4 K-weighting with gating). This is the number they use to turn your song up or down.
True peak
The highest level the waveform reaches between samples, estimated with 4× oversampling, in dBTP. A file can peak above 0 dBTP even when no sample hits 0 dBFS, and those overs can distort after MP3/AAC encoding.
Loudness range
How much the loudness moves between the quiet and loud parts of the song, in LU (EBU Tech 3342). Acoustic songs often have more range than pop, and that's fine.
Clipped spots
Places where 3 or more samples in a row sit at full scale, which usually means the master clipped.

Questions about the free check

Is my file uploaded?

No. The check runs entirely in this browser tab. Your file never leaves your device, and nothing is stored.

Why isn't loudness pass or fail?

Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube and Amazon Music adjust playback volume, so a louder master is simply turned down. What can cause real problems is peaks that are too high, which can distort when the file is converted for streaming.

What LUFS should I aim for on Spotify?

Spotify's default setting plays songs at about −14 LUFS. It recommends a true peak below −1 dBTP, or below −2 dBTP if the master is louder than −14 LUFS. Many acoustic and vocal songs sound good somewhere around −16 to −12 LUFS.

Will my reading match my DAW's meter?

It should be close. The check follows the ITU-R BS.1770-4 standard. Small differences between meters are normal, and mono files can read about 3 LU differently depending on how a meter treats them.

What does the free check not cover?

It measures the file, not the mix. It can't tell you if the vocal is buried or the guitar is muddy. The Porchlight Mix full report covers that.

Sources and notes
  1. Spotify normalizes to −14 LUFS integrated by default and recommends keeping true peak below −1 dBTP, or below −2 dBTP if the master is louder than −14 LUFS. Quieter songs are turned up only as far as leaving 1 dB of peak headroom allows. Spotify for Artists: Loudness normalization.
  2. Apple Music uses Sound Check, but Apple doesn't publish a LUFS target for music. −16 LUFS is the reference level reported by independent measurements (iZotope). Apple recommends leaving at least 1 dB of headroom to avoid clipping on playback (Apple Digital Masters).
  3. YouTube doesn't publish a loudness number. −14 LUFS is the reference level reported by independent measurements, and YouTube is reported to turn loud uploads down but not turn quiet ones up (iZotope). There's no published true-peak limit, so −1 dBTP is shown as general good practice.
  4. Amazon Music offers loudness normalization in its apps (Amazon help) but doesn't publish a target. −14 LUFS comes from independent tests (Production Advice), and −2 dBTP is a conservative working ceiling, not an Amazon rule.
  5. Distributor file specs: DistroKid accepts WAV, FLAC, MP3, M4A, AIFF and WMA, and says a 16-bit, 44.1 kHz WAV is typical (DistroKid help). Other distributors have similar or stricter rules, so check yours. The silence thresholds here are rules of thumb, not published limits.
  6. Measurement: loudness follows ITU-R BS.1770-4 and EBU Tech 3342 (loudness range). Mono files are measured as one channel, as BS.1770 specifies. Some players treat mono as dual-mono, which reads about 3 LU louder. Readings can differ slightly from your DAW's meter. Service targets change over time, and listeners can turn normalization off.
