Why does my song sound quieter on Spotify than other songs?

Porchlight Mix · October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

You finally hear your song on Spotify, and the track that plays next sounds bigger, brighter or simply louder. It's one of the most common questions home recordists ask, and the frustrating answer is that turning your master up usually isn't the fix. Spotify adjusts the playback volume of every track, so "louder" often comes down to things other than the file's level.

Here are the real reasons a song can sound quieter on Spotify, roughly in the order worth checking, and what to do about each one.

First, how Spotify sets the volume

Spotify measures each track's integrated loudness using the ITU-R BS.1770 standard and, on the default setting, adjusts playback so tracks land around −14 LUFS.1 Louder masters are turned down. Quieter masters are turned up, but only as far as their peaks allow: Spotify leaves 1 dB of headroom, so a quiet track with high peaks may not be turned up all the way.1

So in a normalized playlist, a −8 LUFS master and a −14 LUFS master measure about the same at playback. If yours still sounds quieter, something else is going on. Spotify's own artist help page lists several reasons,2 and they're a good place to start.

Reason 1: Your song is quiet and has high peaks

This is the one case where your song really does play at a lower level. If your master measures, say, −18 LUFS with true peaks at −2 dBTP, Spotify can only raise it about 1 dB before it hits the 1 dB headroom limit, so it plays about 3 dB below most other tracks. Spotify gives its own example: a track at −20 LUFS with a true peak of −5 dBFS only gets lifted to −16 LUFS.1

What to do: measure integrated loudness and true peak. If you're well below −14 LUFS and the song isn't meant to be that gentle, a little more limiting or compression can bring it up without hurting it. A few dB of gain reduction on the loudest peaks is often enough for acoustic music. Reason 2: You're comparing different settings or apps

Normalization isn't applied everywhere, the same way:

  • Album vs. playlist. When someone plays a full album, Spotify normalizes the album as a whole, so a soft song stays softer than the loud one next to it. On shuffle or in playlists, each track is adjusted individually.12 If you're comparing your ballad against a different artist's single, you're comparing different situations.
  • Listener settings. Premium listeners can choose Loud (−11 LUFS), Normal (−14) or Quiet (−19).1 On Loud, Spotify raises tracks regardless of peaks and uses a limiter to stop clipping.1
  • Web player and some devices. Spotify says the web player and some third-party devices, like TVs and speakers, don't apply normalization.1 There, louder masters really do play louder.

What to do: compare in the Spotify mobile or desktop app with normalization on, in a playlist alongside a few similar songs.

Reason 3: The other song has more punch at the same loudness

Spotify points out that a very dynamic track mastered around −14 LUFS keeps its peaks, while a heavily limited track at, for example, −6 LUFS gets turned down. Both play at the same measured loudness, but the dynamic track's peaks can feel louder by contrast.2 If you squashed your master to get it loud, it may now sound smaller than a dynamic song at the same level, because it's turned down and its transients are gone.

What to do: try a version with less limiting. For acoustic and vocal music, letting strums and consonants breathe often sounds more alive after normalization than a crushed master.

Reason 4: Frequency balance

LUFS is a good estimate of how loud music feels, not a perfect one. Two common ways the balance tricks it:

  • Too much low end and low mids. The K-weighting filter only partly discounts deep bass, and the low mids count in full, so a mix heavy in boom and low mids gets turned down for energy that doesn't make it sound louder. A boomy acoustic guitar or a mix with built-up low mids can sound dull and small after normalization. See our guide to fixing low-mid buildup.
  • Inaudible high frequencies. Spotify notes that inaudible high-frequency content in a mix can cause the loudness algorithm to overestimate perceived loudness, because BS.1770 doesn't apply a low-pass filter.2 Your track gets turned down for energy nobody hears.

The upper midrange, roughly 2–4 kHz, is where the consonants that make a voice intelligible mostly sit,3 and our ears are very sensitive there. A mix with a clear vocal and a balanced upper midrange often sounds louder than a dull mix at the same LUFS.

What to do: compare your mix with a similar released song at matched loudness. If yours sounds duller or boomier, fix it in the mix with EQ rather than more limiting.

Reason 5: The vocal is buried

Listeners focus on the voice. If your vocal sits lower than in comparable releases, the whole song can seem quieter and farther away even when the overall level matches. Folk and singer-songwriter releases usually feature the vocal clearly, so check that yours is easy to follow at low volume. See how to make a vocal sit on top of an acoustic guitar.

Reason 6: Peaks too hot for encoding

Spotify warns that very loud masters with true peaks above −2 dB can distort during encoding. That distortion can make a track seem louder, but not better.2 Its mastering advice is to keep true peaks below −1 dBTP, or below −2 dBTP if the master is louder than −14 LUFS.1 That isn't a loudness fix, but pushing the limiter to win back loudness often means breaking this rule.

Reason 7: Arrangement and genre

A sparse fingerpicked song and a full band at the same measured loudness won't feel the same. Dense arrangements with lots of sustained energy tend to feel bigger. That's not a problem to fix. If your song is intimate, it's supposed to feel intimate. Compare it with releases in the same style, not with whatever came up next on shuffle.

A quick diagnostic checklist

  • Measure integrated LUFS and true peak. If you're well below −14 LUFS with high peaks, Spotify can't turn you up all the way.
  • Compare in the Spotify app with normalization on, in a playlist, against songs in your genre.
  • Try a less-limited master, and judge it after normalization, not before.
  • Check the low end and low mids for buildup, and filter out inaudible high-frequency noise.
  • Make sure the vocal is clear at low volume.
  • Keep true peaks at −1 dBTP or lower (−2 dBTP if louder than −14 LUFS).
