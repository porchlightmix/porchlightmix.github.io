Why does my acoustic guitar and vocal mix sound muddy? Fixing low-mid buildup

Porchlight Mix · October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

You've got a good performance: a vocal you're happy with and an acoustic guitar that sounded great in the room. But when you play back the mix, it sounds thick and blurry, like a blanket is over the speakers. The vocal doesn't cut through, the guitar sounds boxy, and turning things up only makes it worse.

That's almost always low-mid buildup, and it's the most common problem in simple acoustic mixes. Fixing it is mostly a matter of finding where the extra energy comes from and taking out a little at a time.

What "mud" actually is

When engineers say a mix is muddy, they usually mean too much energy in the low midrange, roughly 200–500 Hz. Nearby there's the "boxy" or "honky" area a little higher, around 300–800 Hz, and the boom region below, around 80–200 Hz. These ranges are rough guides, not exact boundaries. Every voice, guitar and room is different.

The tricky part is that this range isn't bad. It's where the warmth and body of a voice and a guitar live. The problem comes when several sources all pile energy into the same place. Each track can sound fine on its own and still add up to mud.

Where the mud comes from in acoustic recordings

1. Proximity effect

Directional microphones (cardioids, which most vocal and instrument mics are) boost bass as you get closer to the source. This is called proximity effect. Shure notes that a mic like the SM58 can add a significant low-frequency boost when used very close.1 Singing right on the mic, or putting a mic a couple of inches from a guitar, can make both tracks much heavier in the lows and low mids than they sound in the room.

2. Pointing the mic at the soundhole

The soundhole is where the guitar's body resonance comes out, and it's the boomiest spot on the instrument. Sound On Sound's acoustic guitar recording guide describes a common starting point: a mic pointed around where the neck joins the body. Another approach keeps the mic in front of the body but angled away from the soundhole, so it avoids the boom of the guitar's air resonance.2

3. A small, untreated room

Small rooms have resonances (room modes) that boost some bass and low-mid frequencies and cancel others. A vocal or guitar recorded in a small bedroom picks up that uneven low end, which often reads as boxiness or boom on playback.

4. Stacking parts

Double-tracked guitars, a second guitar part, a piano and a bass, or several harmony vocals all contribute low mids. Two or three parts in the same register can easily get crowded.

5. Reverb with too much low end

Reverb tails full of low-mid energy smear across everything. Low end in a reverb rarely adds anything you'd miss, but it adds plenty of blur.

How to fix it, step by step

Start at the source

If you can still re-record, fix it there first. It's easier than EQ.

  • Move the vocalist back a little from the mic, or have them sing slightly off-axis.
  • Move the guitar mic away from the soundhole, toward where the neck joins the body, or angle it off the soundhole, and back it off a bit. Listen on headphones while someone else plays, and move the mic until the guitar sounds balanced.
  • Put soft furnishings (a sofa, a duvet, a rug) around the recording spot to tame reflections in a small room.

Use high-pass filters, gently

A high-pass filter removes energy below a set frequency. On most acoustic-mix tracks there's low end you don't need: rumble, handling noise, proximity-effect boom. Set the filter while listening in the mix, not on solo. Raise the frequency until the track starts to sound thin, then back off a little. Where that lands depends on the voice, the instrument and the arrangement, so trust your ears over any preset number. If there's a bass player in the arrangement, the guitar can usually give up more low end than in a solo guitar-and-vocal song, where the guitar is the bass.

Find the problem frequency with a sweep, then cut a little

  1. On the guitar (or vocal), add an EQ band with a narrow width and boost it by several dB.
  2. Sweep it slowly through roughly 150–600 Hz. When the track suddenly sounds much boomier or boxier, you've found a problem area.
  3. Change that boost into a cut, widen the band, and start small, around 2–3 dB. Then turn the boost off and compare.

Be careful: boosting makes almost every frequency sound bad, so only cut where the change clearly helps when you compare in the full mix. Small, wide cuts usually sound more natural than deep, narrow ones.

Make room for the vocal

Acoustic guitar and vocal share a lot of the same range. Two common moves, used lightly:

  • Complementary EQ: if you cut a little low mid on the guitar, the vocal's warmth comes through without boosting the vocal. If the vocal is unclear, a gentle dip in the guitar in the upper midrange, where vocal intelligibility lives, can help the words cut through without raising the vocal level.
  • Dynamic EQ or a multiband compressor on the vocal can tame low-mid build-up only on the notes or words that cause it, like when the singer leans into the mic, without thinning the whole performance.

Clean up the reverb

Put an EQ on your reverb return and roll off the lows and some low mids. The vocal will still sound spacious, but the space won't cloud the guitar.

Check in mono and on small speakers

Mud often shows up most on laptop speakers, phones and in the car. Switch your mix to mono and listen at low volume. If the vocal disappears into the guitar there, the low mids still need work. A level-matched reference track helps here: pick a released song in a similar style, match its loudness to your mix (a LUFS meter makes this easy), and flip between them. If yours sounds thicker and duller at the same loudness, you have your answer.

Don't overdo it

The risk with fixing mud is going too far: a thin, harsh mix with no body. Some signs you've cut too much:

  • The guitar sounds small and plasticky, or like a cheap pickup.
  • The vocal sounds nasal or loses its chest.
  • The mix sounds clear, but listening is tiring at higher volumes.

If that happens, bring back a dB or two in the low mids, or lower your high-pass filters. The goal is warmth without blur.

A quick muddy-mix checklist

  • Mic placement: off the soundhole, not too close to the vocalist.
  • High-pass filters set by ear in context on every track that doesn't need deep lows.
  • One or two gentle, wide low-mid cuts where a sweep finds a problem, not on every track by default.
  • Space carved for the vocal in the guitar, not just a louder vocal.
  • Reverb returns filtered.
  • Checked in mono, at low volume, and against a level-matched reference.
