How to make a vocal sit on top of an acoustic guitar in the mix

Porchlight Mix · October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

In a song that's mostly voice and acoustic guitar, the two are always competing. Both are in the middle of the stereo image, both have most of their energy in the midrange, and a strummed guitar plays almost constantly. If the vocal keeps getting swallowed in the chorus, or you find yourself turning it up until it sounds pasted on top, the fix is usually a handful of small moves rather than one big one.

Here's a practical order of operations for getting a lead vocal to sit clearly on top of an acoustic guitar while still sounding like it belongs in the same room.

1. Start with balance and the arrangement

Before reaching for plugins, get a static balance with just the faders. In a sparse singer-songwriter arrangement, iZotope suggests the guitar will often sit "just below the volume of the vocal," where you can hear everything the guitar does without it covering the voice.1 Set the vocal level so every word is understandable at a quiet listening level, then bring the guitar up until it supports the vocal without masking it.

Also listen to the parts themselves. If the guitar strums hard in the same register the vocal is singing in, no amount of EQ will fully separate them. Small arrangement changes, like a capo position that moves the guitar voicing away from the melody, lighter strumming under verses, or dropping a doubled guitar under the vocal, are often the most effective fix of all. If you're still tracking, that's the cheapest place to solve it.

2. Clean up the low end and low mids on the guitar

A boomy guitar takes up space the voice needs and makes the whole mix thicker. iZotope's acoustic guitar walkthrough uses a high-pass filter to remove unnatural sub frequencies and a cut to reduce swelling in the low mids, around 200–400 Hz, plus narrow notches on ringing notes.1 These are starting points. Sweep to find what your guitar actually does, and cut gently. Our guide to fixing low-mid buildup walks through the sweep-and-cut method step by step.

3. Make room where the words live

Research on speech shows that the consonants that make words understandable sit mostly in the 2–4 kHz range, and that the band roughly 1–4 kHz is especially important for intelligibility.2 Sung lyrics work the same way. A strummed acoustic guitar also has a lot of energy there, so that's often where the masking happens.

  • Dip the guitar a little in the vocal's presence range. A wide, gentle cut of a dB or two somewhere in the 2–4 kHz area on the guitar can make the words clearer without making the vocal louder. Find the exact spot by ear, in the mix, not on solo.
  • Prefer a dip on the guitar over a big boost on the vocal. Boosting the vocal's upper mids too much quickly sounds harsh, especially on sibilant singers.
  • If the dip makes the guitar dull in instrumental sections, use a dynamic EQ or a sidechained EQ band on the guitar that only dips when the vocal is singing. Many DAWs and EQ plugins can do this.

4. Control the vocal's dynamics

A natural vocal performance moves a lot in level: quiet phrase endings, loud held notes, words that drop away when the singer turns their head. Those quiet words are the ones the guitar buries.

  • Compression evens out the vocal so it stays at a more consistent level against the guitar. Start gently and listen for whether the quiet words stay audible. If the vocal starts sounding squashed or breathy noise jumps up, back off.
  • Volume automation (riding the fader) is often more natural than heavy compression. Push up individual words or phrases that dip, and pull down the ones that jump out. This is the single most effective way to keep a vocal on top through a song.
  • De-essing after any brightening keeps "s" and "t" sounds from becoming painful when the vocal is up front.

5. Compress the guitar to keep it steady

An uneven guitar is hard to balance around: a loud strum covers a word, and then a quiet passage leaves a hole. iZotope suggests gentle compression with a low ratio, slower attack and fast release, with around 2–5 dB of gain reduction, to keep a strummed guitar consistent through the mix. For fingerpicked parts where one note jumps out, a higher threshold and faster attack can catch just that note.1

6. Use panning and space, not just level

  • One guitar: it's common to keep the guitar near the center or just slightly off center for a bit of separation from the vocal.1
  • Two guitars or guitar plus piano: spread them left and right to open the center for the vocal.1
  • Reverb and delay: the guitar can sit a little farther back in the space than the vocal. Keep the vocal's reverb short or filtered so it doesn't blur the words, and use pre-delay so the dry voice speaks first. A single shared room reverb on both helps them sound like one performance.

7. Check it the way listeners will hear it

  • Listen quietly. If you can follow every word at a low volume, the vocal balance is probably right. If you have to turn up to hear the lyrics, the vocal needs more help.
  • Listen in mono and on a small speaker. Phones and smart speakers often play in mono and have little low end. A vocal that's clear in stereo can disappear in mono if the guitar is too wide or too dense.
  • Compare with a reference in your genre at matched loudness. Look at how loud the vocal is relative to the guitar on releases you like, and adjust yours.
  • Take breaks. After hours of mixing, your ears get used to whatever balance you've got. A fresh listen the next morning often reveals a buried vocal immediately.

Common mistakes

  • Turning the vocal up and nothing else. The vocal ends up sounding disconnected and "karaoke-like" while the guitar is still competing for the same range.
  • Over-brightening the vocal to make it cut, so it becomes harsh and sibilant.
  • Scooping too much from the guitar until it sounds thin and small. Small moves on both parts usually beat one big move on one.
  • Mixing on solo. A vocal that sounds great alone can disappear once the guitar comes in. Make your EQ and compression decisions with everything playing.

A quick checklist

  • Static balance set with the vocal clear at low volume
  • Guitar low end filtered and low mids gently cut
  • A small, wide dip on the guitar where the words live (roughly 2–4 kHz, found by ear)
  • Vocal compressed gently, with problem words automated
  • Guitar compressed lightly for consistency
  • Panning and reverb used to give the vocal the front-center spot
  • Checked quietly, in mono, and against a reference
