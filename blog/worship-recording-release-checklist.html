Worship recording release checklist: getting your live set ready for Spotify and Apple Music

Porchlight Mix · October 19, 2026 (updated October 20, 2026) · 6 min read

A live worship set that moved the room can sound very different as a recording on someone's phone. Getting from a multitrack of Sunday's service to songs on Spotify and Apple Music takes a few extra steps: rights, editing, mixing, file prep and metadata. Skipping one is how teams end up with a delayed release, a takedown, or tracks that sound noticeably quieter or rougher than the studio songs around them in a playlist.

Work through this checklist in order. It's written for volunteer and small-church teams, so you don't need to be an engineer to follow it.

1. Sort out the rights first

This is the step most often missed, and it's the one that can get a release pulled.

  • Original songs: make sure everyone who co-wrote them agrees to the release and the songwriter splits, and write that down. You'll need writer names for the metadata anyway.
  • Songs written by other people: a church license for congregational use isn't the same as permission to release a recording. CCLI's Church Copyright License, for example, covers recording services for people who couldn't attend or for your archive,1 but distributing a recording on streaming services is a separate permission. In the US, that's usually a mechanical license for each cover song. Many distributors offer cover-song licensing as an add-on, so ask yours.
  • Backing tracks, loops and multitracks: CCLI notes its recording provision doesn't cover re-recording commercial sound recordings, including backing tracks.1 If your live mix includes tracks you didn't make, check that their license allows a commercial release, or mix them out.

This isn't legal advice. If you're unsure about a specific song, contact the publisher or ask your distributor before you upload.

2. Choose and edit the takes

  • Pick the songs, not the whole service. Releasing a few strong songs is better than releasing everything.
  • Decide where each track starts and ends. On Spotify, someone who plays the whole album hears the songs in order, but a song added to a playlist or heard on shuffle plays by itself. Split tracks at points that sound natural on their own, like the end of a ringing chord, rather than mid-transition.
  • Handle spoken sections deliberately. Prayer and spoken moments can be powerful on an album, but they can work better as their own short track than at the start of a song, where they delay the music for listeners who skip around.
  • Watch the silence. A long gap of near-silence at the start of a track makes listeners think it's broken. Trim the head to the moment the music starts (a brief room-tone fade-in is fine) and fade the tail, crowd and all, to a natural end.

3. Mix for listeners, not the room

A front-of-house mix is built to fill a room that already has drums, a bass amp and a congregation singing in it. On headphones, the same balance usually sounds wrong. Some things to listen for:

  • The lead vocal should stay clear and on top, even in the biggest chorus. Live recordings often lose the vocal when the band builds, so ride it up where it needs it.
  • Crowd and room mics make it feel live. Blend them in, but keep them low enough that they don't wash out the lead vocal and the words.
  • Low end from kick, bass, keys and room boom builds up fast. Check it on earbuds and a small speaker, not just big monitors. If it sounds muddy, see our guide to fixing low-mid buildup.
  • Check in mono. Some phones and smart speakers play in mono. Make sure nothing important disappears.
  • Compare with a reference. Pick a released live worship album you like, match its loudness to your mix, and switch back and forth.

4. Master to the streaming specs

Streaming services turn songs up or down so they play at similar loudness, which is called normalization. That changes what "loud enough" means:

ServiceLoudness referencePeak guidance
Spotify−14 LUFS integrated (default setting)2Below −1 dBTP, or below −2 dBTP if louder than −14 LUFS2
Apple MusicSound Check. No official number, measured around −16 LUFS4At least 1 dB of headroom3
YouTube, Amazon MusicReported around −14 LUFS4No published limit. −1 dBTP is a sensible ceiling

What this means for a worship release:

  • Don't crush it to compete. A master at −8 LUFS gets turned down about 6 dB on Spotify, so it doesn't sound louder there, just flatter. Live worship depends on the build from a quiet verse to a full-band bridge, so protect that.
  • Set a true-peak ceiling of −1 dBTP on your final limiter, or −2 dBTP if the master is on the loud side.
  • Master the songs as a set. Spotify normalizes a whole album together when someone plays the album,2 so balance the songs against each other by ear. A reflective song should feel softer than the anthem by the amount you intend.

5. Export the right files

  • Format: WAV or FLAC. DistroKid, for example, accepts several formats but says a 16-bit, 44.1 kHz WAV is typical.5 Check your distributor's own spec page, since requirements differ.
  • Sample rate: 44.1 kHz or higher. Export at the session's rate or 44.1 kHz rather than upsampling something lower.
  • Stereo, one file per track, named clearly (for example, "01 Song Title.wav").
  • Don't upload MP3s if you have the lossless files. Stores encode your file again, and encoding an already-lossy file loses more quality.
  • Listen to every exported file all the way through before uploading. Glitches, cut-off endings and the wrong mix version are easy to miss.

6. Get the metadata right

  • Artist name: use exactly the same spelling and capitalization on every release, so the music lands on the right artist profile.
  • Song titles as they appear on the original publisher listing for covers, with "Live" versions labeled the way your distributor asks.
  • Songwriter credits for every song, including covers.
  • Cover art: a square image. DistroKid lists 3000 × 3000 pixels.5 Don't include text that breaks store rules, like URLs or social handles.
  • ISRC codes: most distributors assign these automatically. Keep a record of them.

7. Plan the release date

  • Upload early. Give your distributor and the stores time to process the release, and leave room to fix anything that gets flagged.
  • Pitch it on Spotify. Once the release shows up in Spotify for Artists, you can pitch one unreleased song. Spotify says pitching at least 7 days before release lets you pick the song your followers get in Release Radar. One catch for worship teams: Spotify says it doesn't include some alternative versions, like acoustic or live versions, in Release Radar, so a live recording may not show up there.6
  • Tell your church first. Your congregation is your first audience. Announce it from the stage, in the newsletter and on your socials, with a direct link.

The one-page checklist

  • ☐ Rights cleared: originals agreed, covers licensed, backing tracks checked
  • ☐ Takes chosen, track splits sound natural on their own, head/tail silence trimmed
  • ☐ Mix checked on earbuds, in mono, and against a reference
  • ☐ Master: true peak ≤ −1 dBTP (≤ −2 if loud), dynamics intact, songs balanced as a set
  • ☐ Files: stereo WAV/FLAC, 44.1 kHz+, 16- or 24-bit, every file played through
  • ☐ Metadata: consistent artist name, titles, songwriter credits, square cover art
  • ☐ Release date set with time to spare, and a Spotify pitch at least 7 days ahead
