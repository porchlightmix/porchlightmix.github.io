Why did my distributor reject my song? Common reasons and how to fix them before you upload

Porchlight Mix · October 22, 2026 · 6 min read

You finished the song, filled in the upload form, and then got an email saying the release was rejected or flagged. It's discouraging, but usually fixable. Most rejections come down to a handful of things: the cover art, how names and titles are typed, rights to covers or loops, re-used audio, or a problem with the file itself.

Here is each one in plain English, based on what DistroKid, CD Baby, TuneCore and Apple Music publish, plus a checklist for your next upload.

First, read the rejection email closely

Distributors usually tell you what went wrong. DistroKid says the details of a rejection are included in the email you received, and that you can reply to it if you think it was a mistake.1 TuneCore sends flagged releases an email from its Content Review team explaining why, and you need to reply to that email after making the changes so they can review it again. TuneCore says the team gets back to you within 24 business hours.8

DistroKid says a rejected release usually needs to be deleted and uploaded again, except for artwork problems, which you can fix with "Replace Artwork" on the album page.1 Check what your distributor asks for before you delete anything.

1. The cover art breaks a store rule

Artwork is one of the most common problems, and the rules are specific. DistroKid lists things streaming services will reject in album art, including:2

  • Website addresses, QR codes, or a page name on X
  • Social media logos or streaming service logos (like Spotify or iTunes)
  • Prices, or references to physical media like "CD"
  • Blurry, pixelated, rotated or low-quality images
  • Unlicensed or stock photos
  • The same artwork you used on another release

DistroKid asks for a JPG in RGB color, at least 1000 × 1000 pixels, and ideally a perfect square at 3000 × 3000 pixels.2 Apple's Style Guide adds that art must not contain release dates, and must not mention audio formats like "lossless" or "24-bit."9

The text on the cover also has to match what you typed in. CD Baby says the artist name and title on the cover art must be spelled and formatted exactly like your submission, and that anyone shown as "featuring" or "with" on the cover must be credited as a featured artist.6

2. Names and titles don't follow the style rules

Stores want tidy, consistent catalogs, so they check how names and titles are typed. A few rules from the Apple Music Style Guide that catch home artists:9

  • Just the name. Artist names shouldn't include extra info like an instrument, a former band, or a website. "Joe Satriani (Guitarist)" is Apple's own example of what not to do.
  • No generic names. Apple doesn't accept generic artist names or genre names, like "Singer" or "Lo-Fi."
  • One artist per field. Two solo artists on a duet get two separate artist entries, not "Name & Name" in one box. An established duo or band name is the exception.
  • Featured artists go in the featured role, and "feat." in a title is written lowercase, in parentheses or brackets.
  • No extra words in titles. Leave out things like "Exclusive," "Digital Single," "Original Mix," track numbers, or the release year.
  • Normal capitalization. Titles shouldn't be all capitals or all lowercase. English titles use title case.
  • Label versions clearly. Live recordings are marked "Live," and other versions of the same song get version info in parentheses so each title is unique.

Apple's guide is worth following even if you mostly care about Spotify, since one upload often goes to many stores at once.

3. Covers, loops and beats without the right rights

Covers are allowed, but they need a license. DistroKid secures cover licenses through the Harry Fox Agency and says the song you cover must already be released in the US. It also can't contain samples or be a remix, and you can't fundamentally change the original lyrics, title or melody. Genre changes are fine, but translating the lyrics into another language probably isn't. Getting the license can take up to 14 business days.4 On titles, Apple says cover tracks shouldn't mention the original artist with phrases like "Cover of" or "Originally Performed By."9 Apple also says it doesn't accept sound-alike covers that sound very similar to the original.9 Make the song your own.

Loops and beats need care too. CD Baby says your audio may be flagged if it includes content that isn't exclusively yours, and lists royalty-free beats, stock sounds, drum loops, samples, GarageBand loops and leased beats as examples. It may ask for proof that you have the rights.5 Keep your license receipts.

This isn't legal advice. If you're unsure whether a cover or sample is covered, ask your distributor before you upload.

4. The same audio was released before

Uploading the same recording again is riskier than it sounds. CD Baby says audio can be flagged if it was previously distributed with different details, like a different artist name, track title or a missing featured artist credit, even if the earlier release was canceled.5 Apple may hide content that's submitted several times with slightly different titles or artist names.9

Moving a single onto an album? Keep the artist name, title and version info exactly the same. Planning a new mix of an old song? Ask your distributor first.

5. Something is wrong with the file itself

Technical problems are the easiest to avoid. CD Baby asks for stereo WAV or FLAC files at 44.1 kHz and 16-bit (MP3 is accepted but not recommended). File names should use only letters, numbers, hyphens and underscores.7 DistroKid accepts many formats and says a 16-bit, 44.1 kHz WAV is typical. Its file size limit is 1 GB, and it lists characters that can't appear in file names.3

Then listen for what a form can't catch:

  • Wrong version. Make sure it's the final master, not a rough mix.
  • Clipping and distortion. Crackles in loud parts can come from a master pushed too hard. Our guide to how loud an acoustic song should be explains why you don't need to crush it.
  • Long silence. Trim dead air at the start, and let the ending ring out naturally rather than cutting it off. If a silent or hidden track is on purpose, Apple says it has to be labeled in the title, for example "(Silent Track)" or "(Hidden Track)."9

Before-you-upload checklist

  • ☐ Cover art: square, at least 3000 × 3000 if you can, RGB JPG, no URLs, handles, logos, prices or release dates, and not used on another release
  • ☐ Artist name and title on the cover match the form exactly
  • ☐ Artist name is just the name, spelled the same as on past releases
  • ☐ Titles in normal title case, with no extra words, and featured artists in the featured role
  • ☐ Covers licensed, and loops, beats and samples cleared, with receipts saved
  • ☐ Re-used recordings keep the same artist, title and version info as before
  • ☐ Final master, in the format your distributor asks for, with a simple file name
  • ☐ Played all the way through: no clipping, no dead air, no cut-off ending
