How to record vocals and acoustic guitar at the same time (and manage bleed)

Porchlight Mix · October 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Lots of singer-songwriters play better when they sing and play at the same time. The timing breathes, the dynamics follow the lyric, and the song feels like a performance instead of a stack of overdubs. The catch is bleed: the vocal mic picks up guitar, and the guitar mic picks up voice. Handled badly, bleed causes a hollow, phasey sound, and you lose the ability to fix either part on its own.

The good news is that bleed isn't something to eliminate completely. It's something to manage. Here are your options, from simplest to most controlled, plus what to watch for when you mix.

First decide: one take, or separate tracks?

Ask yourself one question: do you need to edit or tune the vocal and guitar separately?

  • If you're happy to keep the performance mostly as played, recording both at once is a great choice. You'll mix it more like a live recording.
  • If you expect to comp vocals from several takes, fix pitch heavily, or replace guitar sections, it's usually easier to record separately, or record together and treat that as a guide track you replace later.

The reason: with bleed, every edit or effect on one track also affects the copy of that source in the other mic. Pitch correction on the vocal, for example, also processes the guitar that leaked into the vocal mic, and the two copies of the guitar no longer match.

Option 1: One microphone

The simplest setup is a single mic placed to capture a balanced blend of voice and guitar, usually a little in front of the player at about chest height, so it hears the mouth and the guitar at similar levels. Recording Magazine notes that many classic folk recordings were made this way with a single omnidirectional mic.1

Pros: no phase problems between mics, a natural sound, quick to set up.
Cons: you set the balance between voice and guitar with mic position and the performance, and you can't change it much later. The room becomes part of the sound, so this works best in a room that sounds good.

To adjust the balance, move the mic up toward the mouth for more vocal or down toward the guitar for more guitar, and record short tests until it sounds right on playback.

Option 2: Two directional mics, aimed carefully

This is the most common home setup: one mic on the voice and one on the guitar, usually cardioids. Each cardioid is least sensitive directly behind it, so aim the back of each mic toward the source you don't want:

  • Vocal mic: at mouth height or just above, pointing at the mouth, so its back points down and away from the guitar as much as the angles allow.
  • Guitar mic: aimed at the guitar around where the neck joins the body, a short distance away. That's also a good spot for avoiding soundhole boom. Angle it so the singer's mouth is as far off-axis as possible.

Sound On Sound's Studio SOS team worked through exactly this problem in a reader's home studio. A few practical things helped them: playing seated on a tall stool so the guitar sat lower and the performer moved less, putting the vocal mic just above mouth level, putting the guitar mic around 8 inches away aimed near the neck joint, and adding rough acoustic treatment (upended mattresses) to reduce room reflections.2 They also note the ideal is to space the mics much farther apart than each mic is from its source, which is hard when the mouth and guitar are so close together. That's why angles and room treatment matter so much.2

Hypercardioid mics reject more from the sides than cardioids, with their least sensitive angle toward the back rather than straight behind. Recording Magazine suggests them as a good second choice when figure-8 mics aren't available.1

Option 3: Two figure-8 mics

A figure-8 (bidirectional) mic picks up from the front and back but very little from the sides. Point the side of the vocal mic at the guitar, and the side of the guitar mic at the mouth, and you can noticeably reduce bleed. LEWITT describes this setup, with both mics about 5–8 inches from their sources.3 Recording Magazine calls the figure-8 "the tool for the job" when you need to separate guitar and vocal.1

Two cautions from people who've measured it:

  • Room reflections: a figure-8 also picks up from the back, so the room behind it matters. The SOS team hung a duvet behind the mics.2
  • Don't expect total isolation: in the SOS session, figure-8s reduced voice in the guitar mic by at least 6 dB and guitar in the vocal mic by about 3–4 dB compared with their cardioid setup. That was enough to reduce phasing and make the balance easier, but not total separation, partly because a guitar body is too big to fit inside the narrow null. They found a few degrees of mic angle made a difference of several dB.2

If you own multi-pattern condenser mics, this is worth trying. If you don't, cardioids aimed carefully plus a treated corner will get you most of the way.

Option 4: A stereo pair

Another approach is to skip close-miking and record the whole performance with a stereo pair (for example, two matched small-diaphragm mics in an X/Y or ORTF setup) a little distance away. Like the single-mic option, you set the balance with placement and performance, but you get a natural stereo image with no phase problems between separate close mics.1

Setup tips that help with every option

  • Treat the space around you. Blankets, a duvet, a mattress or a sofa near the performer cut the reflections that carry sound from one source into the other mic.
  • Monitor on closed-back headphones so the headphone mix doesn't leak into the mics.
  • Record a short test and listen with both mics combined in mono. If the guitar sounds thin or hollow when you add the vocal mic, adjust the mic angles or distances. Try flipping the polarity on one track to see whether the combination sounds fuller.
  • Leave headroom. Strummed accents and hand slaps can peak well above the average level. In the SOS session, rhythmic slaps peaked 12–15 dB over the rest of the guitar.2 Set your input gain for the loudest moments, not the average.

Mixing a recording with bleed

  • Treat the two mics as a pair. Set the balance with both faders up and listen to the combined sound, not each track on solo.
  • Go easy on effects that change pitch or timing on one mic only, like heavy tuning, time-stretching or big delays, because they create a mismatched copy of the other source.
  • EQ with both mics playing. A guitar mic that sounds thin on its own can sound right once the guitar bleed in the vocal mic is added, as the SOS team found.2
  • Use one shared reverb for both, so the bleed and the direct sound sit in the same space.
